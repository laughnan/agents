# Meaning-First Writing

## Why this skill exists

LLM prose has recognizable habits: the em-dash pivot, the "not X, but Y" contrast, the triadic flourish. These are not stylistic choices; they are high-probability continuations, and banning them mid-draft produces stilted writing because the prohibition competes with meaning-making. Orwell diagnosed the underlying disease in 1946: ready-made phrases that "will construct your sentences for you — even think your thoughts for you." His cure is the method here: **let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about.** Draft free, then revise hard.

Orwell supplies the core method. Four other masters each contribute one mechanism he lacks, marked where they appear. The writer's own rules, at the end, outrank everything else in this file.

## The method: draft, then revise

**Pass 1 — draft for meaning.** Before writing, get the meaning clear wordlessly: picture the concrete thing you are describing, know what the reader should believe or do afterward. Take Pinker's classic-style stance: you have seen something the reader has not, and your job is to point so they see it for themselves. Draft naturally, in a voice that sounds like the owner (Vonnegut). Do not police style while drafting.

**Pass 2 — revise sentence by sentence.** For each sentence, ask Orwell's questions:

1. What am I trying to say?
2. What words will express it?
3. What image or example will make it clearer?
4. Is it fresh enough to have an effect?

Then the two that do most of the work: **Could I put it more shortly? Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?**

And one Orwell cannot ask, because it is about the reader rather than the words (Pinker's curse of knowledge): **What does this sentence assume the reader already knows?** Read each claim as the least-informed person who will plausibly receive the document. Every unexplained acronym and abstraction-where-an-example-belongs is the curse at work.

**Revision target (King):** aim to return the draft about 10% shorter than it arrived.

**Final sweep:** reread the revised draft once, checking only the writer-provided rules below. They describe patterns that survive sentence-level revision because they live between sentences.

## The habits

1. **Choose every phrase.** If a construction arrived pre-assembled, pause and hunt for the words that fit *this* meaning. Keep it only if, on inspection, it is the right one.
2. **Prefer the concrete.** Orwell's Ecclesiastes test: "race, battle, bread" beats "success in competitive activities." Give every abstract claim one concrete anchor.
3. **Prefer the short word and the everyday word.**
4. **Give every sentence an actor.** Someone does something.
5. **Cut every word that can be cut.**
6. **Reach for the period first.** Then the colon or semicolon. The em-dash comes last and rarely.
7. **State the positive claim and let it stand.** Most contrasts exist to make the writer sound decisive, not to inform.
8. **Sound like the owner, not like prose in general** (Vonnegut). A style pass that sands the voice off a document has failed.

## The finish line (Ogilvy)

- **Read it aloud.** Anything you would not say to the recipient across a table, rewrite.
- **Make it crystal clear what you want the recipient to do.** If the document has no ask, that is a finding about the document.
- **Scope check:** he capped memos at two pages. When a draft runs long, ask whether the length serves the reader or the writer.

## Writer-provided rules

Authored by the owner, extended over time. These outrank the masters and the habits above. Check them in the final sweep, and when any conflict, these win.

1. Don't build a straw man to knock down. Use "not X, it's Y" once per piece, max. (And none per piece if possible.)
2. Two examples are enough. Don't stretch to three.
3. Don't announce what you're about to say. Say it.
4. Don't end two subsequent paragraphs with punchlines.
5. Vary the length and shape of neighboring sentences if more than 2 in a row follow a specific pattern.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than write like a machine.

## What this skill does not govern

Commitment calibration ("we will demonstrate" vs. "we can test"), product facts, and claim strength are content decisions, not style. Leave those exactly as drafted unless asked; if one looks risky, flag it in a note rather than silently softening it.
